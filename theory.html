Gaussian Random Vectors

A Gaussian random variable (or Normal random variable) is a continuous random variable with a bell-shaped probability distribution. It is one of the most widely used distributions in probability and statistics due to the **Central Limit Theorem, which states that the sum of many independent random variables tends to follow a normal distribution, regardless of the original distributions of the variables. Therefore, a simple Gaussian assumption for a naturally occuring distribution often tends to provide accurate analysis of the system. We define and study both 1-d and 2-d Gaussian Random Variables in this experiment.

1D (Univariate) Gaussian Random Variable

1 Definition

A 1D Gaussian random variable XXX denoted as X∼N(μ,σ2)X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)X∼N(μ,σ2), is characterized by two parameters:

  • Mean μ\muμ (center of the distribution)
  • Variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 (spread of the distribution)


Figure 1: Probability Density Function of a 1D Gaussian Random Variable

The probability density function (PDF) of XXX is given by:

fX(x)=12πσ2exp⁡(−(x−μ)22σ2) \begin{equation} f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \end{equation} fX​(x)=2πσ2​1​exp(−2σ2(x−μ)2​)​​

2 Properties

  1. Symmetry: The normal distribution is symmetric around its mean μ\muμ.
  2. Standard Normal Distribution: When μ=0\mu = 0μ=0 and σ2=1\sigma^2 = 1σ2=1, the Gaussian random variable is called a **standard normal variable, denoted by Z∼N(0,1)Z \sim N(0, 1)Z∼N(0,1). Its PDF is:

fZ(z)=12πexp⁡(−z22) \begin{equation} f_Z(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{z^2}{2} \right) \end{equation} fZ​(z)=2π​1​exp(−2z2​)​​

  1. Transformation of a Gaussian Random Variable:
    • If X∼N(μ,σ2)X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)X∼N(μ,σ2), and we transform Y=aX+bY = aX + bY=aX+b, then Y∼N(aμ+b,a2σ2)Y \sim N(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)Y∼N(aμ+b,a2σ2).

3 Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

The CDF of a standard normal variable is denoted by Φ(x)\Phi(x)Φ(x), which is the probability that X≤xX \leq xX≤x:

P(X≤x)=Φ(x−μσ)whereΦ(x)=12π∫−∞xe−t22⋅dt \begin{equation} P(X \leq x) = \Phi\left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right) \quad \text{where} \quad \Phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{\frac{-t^2}{2}}\cdot dt \end{equation} P(X≤x)=Φ(σx−μ​)whereΦ(x)=2π​1​∫−∞x​e2−t2​⋅dt​​

However, there is no closed-form expression for Φ(x)\Phi(x)Φ(x); it is generally computed numerically.

4 Properties of CDF

Here are some properties of the Φ\PhiΦ function that can be shown from its definition.

  2. Φ(0)=12\Phi(0)=\frac{1}{2}Φ(0)=21​;
  3. Φ(−x)=1−Φ(x)\Phi(-x)=1-\Phi(x)Φ(−x)=1−Φ(x), for all x∈Rx \in \mathbb{R}x∈R.
  4. About 68% of values drawn from a normal distribution are within one standard deviation σ from the mean; about 95% of the values lie within two standard deviations; and about 99.7% are within three standard deviations. More precisely, the probability that a normal deviate lies in the range between μ−nσ\mu -n \sigmaμ−nσ and μ+nσ\mu + n \sigmaμ+nσ is given by

F(μ+nσ)−F(μ−nσ)=Φ(n)−Φ(−n)=2Φ(n)−1 \begin{equation} F(\mu+n \sigma)-F(\mu-n \sigma)=\Phi(n)-\Phi(-n)=2 \Phi(n)-1 \end{equation} F(μ+nσ)−F(μ−nσ)=Φ(n)−Φ(−n)=2Φ(n)−1​​

5 Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The CLT roughly states that the sum (or average) of a large number of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables tends to be normally distributed, even if the original variables are not normal. We will discuss about CLT in detail in CLT Experiment

2D Gaussian Random Variable (Bivariate Normal Distribution)

1 Definition

A bivariate normal distribution describes two jointly normal random variables XXX and YYY with the following parameters:

  • Means: μX\mu_XμX​, μY\mu_YμY​
  • Variances: σX2\sigma_X^2σX2​, σY2\sigma_Y^2σY2​
  • Covariance: σXY=E[(X−μX)⋅(Y−μY)]\sigma_{XY} = E\left[(X-\mu_X)\cdot(Y-\mu_Y)\right]σXY​=E[(X−μX​)⋅(Y−μY​)]

We denote this as:

(XY)∼N((μXμY),(σX2σXYσXYσY2)) \begin{equation} \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \end{pmatrix} \sim N\left( \begin{pmatrix} \mu_X \\ \mu_Y \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_X^2 & \sigma_{XY} \\ \sigma_{XY} & \sigma_Y^2 \end{pmatrix} \right) \end{equation} (XY​)∼N((μX​μY​​),(σX2​σXY​​σXY​σY2​​))​​

2 Joint Probability Density Function (PDF)

The joint probability density function (PDF) of XXX and YYY is given by:

fX,Y(x,y)=12πσXσY1−ρ2exp⁡(−12(1−ρ2)[(x−μX)2σX2+(y−μY)2σY2−2ρ(x−μX)(y−μY)σXσY]) \begin{equation} f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sigma_X \sigma_Y \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \left[ \frac{(x - \mu_X)^2}{\sigma_X^2} + \frac{(y - \mu_Y)^2}{\sigma_Y^2} - \frac{2\rho(x - \mu_X)(y - \mu_Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} \right] \right) \end{equation} fX,Y​(x,y)=2πσX​σY​1−ρ2​1​exp(−2(1−ρ2)1​[σX2​(x−μX​)2​+σY2​(y−μY​)2​−σX​σY​2ρ(x−μX​)(y−μY​)​])​​

Where:

  • μX\mu_XμX​, μY\mu_YμY​ are the means of XXX and YYY
  • σX2\sigma_X^2σX2​, σY2\sigma_Y^2σY2​ are the variances of XXX and YYY
  • ρ\rhoρ is the correlation coefficient between XXX and YYY, given by ρ=σXYσXσY\rho = \frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}ρ=σX​σY​σXY​​

3 Key Properties of the Bivariate Normal Distribution

  1. Marginal Distributions:

    • The marginal distribution of XXX is X∼N(μX,σX2)X \sim N(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2)X∼N(μX​,σX2​).
    • The marginal distribution of YYY is Y∼N(μY,σY2)Y \sim N(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2)Y∼N(μY​,σY2​).
    • This means that each random variable XXX and YYY follows a normal distribution independently, but their joint behavior is governed by the covariance or correlation.

  2. Independence:

    • If ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0, then XXX and YYY are independent. In other words, zero correlation implies independence for jointly normal random variables.

    • Example:

      • Let XXX and YYY be independent random variables with means 000 and standard deviations 111. This means ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0. Then the joint PDF simplifies to:

      fX,Y(x,y)=12πexp⁡(−12[x2+y2]) f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ x^2 + y^2 \right] \right) fX,Y​(x,y)=2π1​exp(−21​[x2+y2])

      This is simply the product of two univariate standard normal PDFs.

  3. Conditional Distribution:

    • The conditional distribution of YYY given X=xX = xX=x is normal, with the following parameters:

      • Mean: μY∣X=μY+ρσYσX(x−μX)\mu_{Y|X} = \mu_Y + \rho \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X)μY∣X​=μY​+ρσX​σY​​(x−μX​)
      • Variance: σY∣X2=(1−ρ2)σY2\sigma_{Y|X}^2 = (1 - \rho^2) \sigma_Y^2σY∣X2​=(1−ρ2)σY2​

    • Example:

      • Suppose XXX and YYY are normally distributed with μX=2\mu_X = 2μX​=2, μY=3\mu_Y = 3μY​=3, σX=1\sigma_X = 1σX​=1, σY=2\sigma_Y = 2σY​=2, and ρ=0.5\rho = 0.5ρ=0.5. If X=3X = 3X=3, then the conditional distribution of YYY given X=3X = 3X=3 is:

      Y∣X=3∼N(3+0.5×21(3−2),(1−0.52)×22) \begin{equation} Y | X = 3 \sim N\left( 3 + 0.5 \times \frac{2}{1}(3 - 2), (1 - 0.5^2) \times 2^2 \right) \end{equation} Y∣X=3∼N(3+0.5×12​(3−2),(1−0.52)×22)​​

      Simplifying:

      Y∣X=3∼N(4,3) \begin{equation} Y | X = 3 \sim N(4, 3) \end{equation} Y∣X=3∼N(4,3)​​

      Therefore, YYY given X=3X = 3X=3 is normally distributed with a mean of 4 and variance of 3.

  4. Covariance Matrix:

    • The covariance matrix Σ\SigmaΣ for a bivariate normal distribution summarizes the variances and covariances of the random variables:

      Σ=(σX2σXYσXYσY2) \begin{equation} \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_X^2 & \sigma_{XY} \\ \sigma_{XY} & \sigma_Y^2 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation} Σ=(σX2​σXY​​σXY​σY2​​)​​

    • In terms of the correlation coefficient ρ\rhoρ, the covariance σXY\sigma_{XY}σXY​ is given by:

      σXY=ρσXσY \begin{equation} \sigma_{XY} = \rho \sigma_X \sigma_Y \end{equation} σXY​=ρσX​σY​​​

      Therefore, the covariance matrix can also be expressed as:

      Σ=(σX2ρσXσYρσXσYσY2) \begin{equation} \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_X^2 & \rho \sigma_X \sigma_Y \\ \rho \sigma_X \sigma_Y & \sigma_Y^2 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation} Σ=(σX2​ρσX​σY​​ρσX​σY​σY2​​)​​

Example: Computing Covariance Matrix and Joint PDF

Let XXX and YYY be jointly normal with the following parameters:

  • μX=1\mu_X = 1μX​=1, μY=2\mu_Y = 2μY​=2
  • σX=1\sigma_X = 1σX​=1, σY=2\sigma_Y = 2σY​=2
  • ρ=0.6\rho = 0.6ρ=0.6

  1. Covariance Matrix: Using σXY=ρσXσY=0.6×1×2=1.2\sigma_{XY} = \rho \sigma_X \sigma_Y = 0.6 \times 1 \times 2 = 1.2σXY​=ρσX​σY​=0.6×1×2=1.2, the covariance matrix is:

    Σ=(11.21.24) \begin{equation} \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation} Σ=(11.2​1.24​)​​

  2. Joint PDF: The joint PDF is:

    fX,Y(x,y)=12π⋅1⋅2⋅1−0.62exp⁡(−12(1−0.62)[(x−1)212+(y−2)222−2⋅0.6⋅(x−1)(y−2)1⋅2]) \begin{equation} f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot \sqrt{1 - 0.6^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2(1 - 0.6^2)} \left[ \frac{(x - 1)^2}{1^2} + \frac{(y - 2)^2}{2^2} - \frac{2 \cdot 0.6 \cdot (x - 1)(y - 2)}{1 \cdot 2} \right] \right) \end{equation} fX,Y​(x,y)=2π⋅1⋅2⋅1−0.62​1​exp(−2(1−0.62)1​[12(x−1)2​+22(y−2)2​−1⋅22⋅0.6⋅(x−1)(y−2)​])​​

    Simplifying the coefficient in front:

    12π⋅2⋅1−0.36=14π⋅0.64=14π⋅0.8=13.2π \begin{equation} \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 2 \cdot \sqrt{1 - 0.36}} = \frac{1}{4\pi \cdot \sqrt{0.64}} = \frac{1}{4\pi \cdot 0.8} = \frac{1}{3.2\pi} \end{equation} 2π⋅2⋅1−0.36​1​=4π⋅0.64​1​=4π⋅0.81​=3.2π1​​​

    Therefore, the joint PDF is:

    fX,Y(x,y)=13.2πexp⁡(−11.28[(x−1)2+(y−2)24−0.6(x−1)(y−2)]) \begin{equation} f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{3.2\pi} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{1.28} \left[ (x - 1)^2 + \frac{(y - 2)^2}{4} - 0.6(x - 1)(y - 2) \right] \right) \end{equation} fX,Y​(x,y)=3.2π1​exp(−1.281​[(x−1)2+4(y−2)2​−0.6(x−1)(y−2)])​​

4 Linear Combinations of Gaussian Random Variables

A key result of the bivariate normal distribution is that any linear combination of XXX and YYY, say Z=aX+bYZ = aX + bYZ=aX+bY, is also normally distributed.

  • The mean of ZZZ is: μZ=aμX+bμY\mu_Z = a\mu_X + b\mu_YμZ​=aμX​+bμY​
  • The variance of ZZZ is: σZ2=a2σX2+b2σY2+2abσXY\sigma_Z^2 = a^2 \sigma_X^2 + b^2 \sigma_Y^2 + 2ab \sigma_{XY}σZ2​=a2σX2​+b2σY2​+2abσXY​

Example:

Let XXX and YYY be normally distributed with the following parameters:

  • μX=1\mu_X = 1μX​=1, μY=2\mu_Y = 2μY​=2
  • σX=1\sigma_X = 1σX​=1, σY=2\sigma_Y = 2σY​=2
  • ρ=0.5\rho = 0.5ρ=0.5, σXY=1\sigma_{XY} = 1σXY​=1

Now consider Z=X+2YZ = X + 2YZ=X+2Y.

  1. Mean:

    μZ=μX+2μY=1+2×2=5 \begin{equation} \mu_Z = \mu_X + 2\mu_Y = 1 + 2 \times 2 = 5 \end{equation} μZ​=μX​+2μY​=1+2×2=5​​

  2. Variance:

    σZ2=12×12+22×22+2×1×2×1=1+16+4=21 \begin{equation} \sigma_Z^2 = 1^2 \times 1^2 + 2^2 \times 2^2 + 2 \times 1 \times 2 \times 1 = 1 + 16 + 4 = 21 \end{equation} σZ2​=12×12+22×22+2×1×2×1=1+16+4=21​​

Thus, Z∼N(5,21)Z \sim N(5, 21)Z∼N(5,21).

Contours

A simple and intuituve way to visualize and understand bi-variate gaussian random variables is by contours (also called level curves). Formally, for a function fff, contours are defined as a set of points given by

{x∈R2:f(x)=c}for some c∈R \begin{equation} \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : f(x)=c\} \quad \text{for some $c \in \mathbb{R}$} \end{equation} {x∈R2:f(x)=c}for some c∈R​​

Now we study the shape of contours, which will be helpful in visualizing 2D-Gaussian Random Variables' distribution. In order to obtain the shape of the contours, we need to solve the equation p(x;μ,Σ)=cp(x;\mu,\Sigma)=cp(x;μ,Σ)=c for some constant c∈Rc \in \mathbb{R}c∈R.

p(x;μ,Σ)=12πσ1σ2exp⁡(−12σ12(x1−μ1)2−12σ22(x2−μ2)2) \begin{equation} p(x ; \mu, \Sigma)=\frac{1}{2 \pi \sigma_1 \sigma_2} \exp \left(-\frac{1}{2 \sigma_1^2}\left(x_1-\mu_1\right)^2-\frac{1}{2 \sigma_2^2}\left(x_2-\mu_2\right)^2\right) \end{equation} p(x;μ,Σ)=2πσ1​σ2​1​exp(−2σ12​1​(x1​−μ1​)2−2σ22​1​(x2​−μ2​)2)​​

Defining the following parameters for p(x;μ,Σ)=cp(x;\mu,\Sigma) = cp(x;μ,Σ)=c, where c∈Rc \in \mathbb{R}c∈R

r1=2σ12log⁡(12πcσ1σ2)r2=2σ22log⁡(12πcσ1σ2) \begin{equation} r_1=\sqrt{2 \sigma_1^2 \log \left(\frac{1}{2 \pi c \sigma_1 \sigma_2}\right)} \quad r_2=\sqrt{2 \sigma_2^2 \log \left(\frac{1}{2 \pi c \sigma_1 \sigma_2}\right)} \end{equation} r1​=2σ12​log(2πcσ1​σ2​1​)​r2​=2σ22​log(2πcσ1​σ2​1​)​​​

it follows that

1=(x1−μ1r1)2+(x2−μ2r2)2 \begin{equation} 1=\left(\frac{x_1-\mu_1}{r_1}\right)^2+\left(\frac{x_2-\mu_2}{r_2}\right)^2 \end{equation} 1=(r1​x1​−μ1​​)2+(r2​x2​−μ2​​)2​​

The obtained equation is that of an axis-aligned ellipse, with center (μ1,μ2)\left(\mu_1, \mu_2\right)(μ1​,μ2​), where the x1x_1x1​ axis has length 2r12 r_12r1​ and the x2x_2x2​ axis has length 2r22 r_{2}2r2​. Thus, contours in gaussian random vectors are ellipses.

Note that when σ1=σ2\sigma_1=\sigma_2σ1​=σ2​, we have r1=r2r_1=r_2r1​=r2​ and thus, the ellipse reduces to a circle. Also, it is interesting to note that the principal axis of the ellipse determines the covariance between the 2 marginal distributions XXX and YYY. A positive value of ρ\rhoρ means a positive slope and a negative value indicates a negative slope.
